## Prologue · Dawn Above the Sea of Clouds

At dawn, the tip of Lotus Peak rises out of the sea of clouds like an island just surfaced from the deep.

Legend holds that the Yellow Emperor once refined his elixir here and ascended to heaven — and so the mountain is called Huangshan. From that day on, these seventy-two strange peaks, with their pines and rocks carved from granite over hundreds of millions of years, became in the Chinese mind the very answer to the word "shanshui" — mountain-and-water.

## The Hundred Million Years of Granite

The story of Huangshan is written in rock.

Hundreds of millions of years ago granite rose here; then glaciers and running water took turns carving it into what we see today — the "four wonders" of odd pines, strange rocks, cloud seas and hot springs. Lotus Peak, Bright Summit and Celestial Capital stand as a tripod, with Lotus Peak — the highest, at 1,864.8 metres — in the lead. Rocks as brushes, pines as ink, clouds as paper: nature painted her own landscape scroll in the heart of Anhui.

## The Yellow Emperor's Furnace

Legend says the Yellow Emperor once refined his elixir in these mountains and ascended to heaven — hence the name Huangshan.

The legend cannot be verified, but a mountain with a name acquires a memory. The Greeting Pine reaches out an arm to welcome every visitor; rocks such as "Monkey Watching the Sea" and "Dreaming Pen Blooms a Flower" are uncannily lifelike — generation after generation lends the rocks its imagination, and the rocks give the story back to the traveller.

## Xu Xiake's Two Ascents

Between 1616 and 1618, the great traveller Xu Xiake climbed Huangshan twice.

He walked every strange peak, watched every sea of cloud, and left one verdict: "Of all the famous mountains within and beyond the seas, none can match Huangshan of Huizhou." Word of mouth later turned it into the saying "having seen Huangshan, no sacred mountain can impress" — from that time on, Huangshan became the "highest standard" of Chinese mountain scenery.

## The Painters' Mountain

In the late Ming and early Qing, painters such as Jian Jiang, Mei Qing and Shi Tao lived in the mountains for long periods sketching, taking Huangshan as their master and founding the "Huangshan school."

They wrote its pines, rocks and cloud seas into the history of Chinese art: every peak and ravine fit for a painting, every rock and pine possessed of temperament. Huangshan ceased to be merely a mountain on the map — it became the spiritual homeland of Chinese landscape painting. The painters' Huangshan is more immortal than stone.

## 1990: The Road to a Double Heritage

In 1990, Mount Huangshan was inscribed on the World Heritage List under criteria (ii), (vii) and (x) as a mixed cultural and natural heritage; in 2004 it was also named a UNESCO Global Geopark.

Culture lives in the paintings, nature lives in the mountains — Huangshan claimed both at once. Today the fame of "having seen Huangshan, no sacred mountain can impress," together with the resilience of the Huangshan pine taking root on sheer cliffs, has long been a symbol of Chinese mountain culture.

## Epilogue · The Return

Having seen the Five Sacred Mountains, no other mountain can impress; having seen Huangshan, no sacred mountain can.

How many people climb with this saying in mind, and keep it as a memory for life the moment the clouds part? A hundred million years of rock, paintings and poems of every age — Huangshan never speaks; it only stands there, waiting for visitors, waiting for the clouds to open, waiting for one word: "worth it."

## Outstanding Universal Value (OUV)

- **Criterion (ii)**: Artistic traditions inspired by Huangshan, such as the Huangshan school of painting, profoundly influenced Chinese landscape painting and garden art.
- **Criterion (vii)**: The landscape of pines, rocks, cloud seas and springs is the supreme expression of natural beauty in Chinese mountains.
- **Criterion (x)**: Huangshan preserves rich vertical biodiversity and is a habitat for many rare species.
